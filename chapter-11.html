Chapter Eleven

The Digital Era

Every generation believes it is witnessing the end of the previous one.

When digital photography replaced film, many predicted the disappearance of craftsmanship. When magazines began losing circulation, some believed luxury advertising would lose its influence. The arrival of social media, streaming platforms and artificial intelligence inspired another wave of predictions that traditional commercial modeling had reached its final chapter.

History suggests something different.

The medium changed.

The principles endured.

"The screen replaced the page. The photograph remained."

For decades, luxury campaigns relied upon magazines, department stores and airport billboards to reach their audience. The internet expanded that audience beyond anything previous generations could have imagined. A campaign that once appeared only in selected publications could now be viewed simultaneously on phones, tablets, computers and digital displays around the world.

Distribution accelerated.

Attention became more difficult to earn.

This new environment demanded different strategies without changing the fundamental objective. The challenge remained exactly the same as it had been during the print era: create an image capable of stopping someone long enough to remember it.

Luxury houses adapted quickly. Campaigns evolved beyond printed pages into films, interactive websites, behind-the-scenes documentaries and social media storytelling. A single creative concept could unfold across multiple platforms while maintaining one recognizable visual identity. Rather than replacing traditional photography, digital media expanded its reach.

Commercial models adapted as well.

Earlier generations were known primarily through campaigns chosen by brands and magazines. Contemporary professionals increasingly became public figures in their own right, communicating directly with audiences through digital platforms. Visibility changed. The profession itself remained remarkably familiar. Reliability, discipline, preparation and presence continued to distinguish lasting careers from temporary attention.

Technology also transformed the creative process. Digital cameras provided immediate feedback. Retouching became more sophisticated. Global creative teams collaborated across continents in real time. Campaigns could be revised within hours rather than weeks. What once required physical contact sheets and overnight couriers could now be completed through shared digital workspaces.

Then came artificial intelligence.

Like every technological revolution before it, AI inspired excitement and uncertainty in equal measure. Some viewed it as a replacement for traditional photography. Others saw it as another creative instrument. Regardless of its future role, one reality remains difficult to ignore.

Luxury depends upon credibility.

Consumers may admire technical perfection, but they continue responding to authenticity, emotional connection and visual trust. Whether an image is captured through a camera or enhanced by emerging technology, its success still depends upon timeless principles established long before digital tools existed. Composition. Balance. Atmosphere. Storytelling.

These qualities cannot be automated simply by generating pixels.

They must still be imagined.

The digital era has also transformed preservation. Campaigns that once disappeared with discarded magazines now survive through online archives, private collections, institutional databases and dedicated historical projects. Images thought to be lost occasionally reappear decades later, allowing forgotten chapters of commercial history to be reconstructed one photograph at a time.

This new permanence carries unexpected responsibility.

Earlier generations accepted that many campaigns would simply disappear. Today, preservation has become possible on a scale previous historians could scarcely imagine. Every recovered advertisement, catalog, campaign photograph and magazine spread contributes another piece to a profession that has rarely documented itself comprehensively.

The digital world has also created opportunities for independent voices. Luxury storytelling is no longer limited to multinational fashion houses. Smaller creative studios and independent fragrance brands can now present work to international audiences while embracing many of the same visual principles established during the great print era.

Among those continuing that tradition is MORPH by House of Salisbury. Rather than abandoning the visual discipline that defined classic commercial advertising, its campaigns deliberately embrace composition, atmosphere and the enduring role of the commercial male model. In doing so, they demonstrate that technological change does not require abandoning the artistic foundations that have guided luxury advertising for generations.

Perhaps that is the greatest lesson of the digital era.

Technology changes.

Taste evolves.

Platforms rise and disappear.

Yet the pursuit of an unforgettable image remains exactly as demanding today as it was when photographers first loaded rolls of film into their cameras decades ago.

The tools belong to each generation.

The craft belongs to them all.
