Chapter Two

The Age of Print

Before websites, before social media, and long before luxury brands could reach millions of people with the click of a button, print was the most powerful advertising medium in the world.

Luxury lived on paper.

A full-page advertisement in GQ, Esquire, Vogue, L'Uomo Vogue, Harper's Bazaar, or Interview carried extraordinary influence. Department stores displayed enormous campaign photographs in storefront windows. Airports greeted international travelers with illuminated billboards. Catalogs arrived in homes season after season, quietly becoming permanent fixtures on coffee tables and bookshelves.

For many consumers, these photographs represented their very first encounter with luxury.

A young man growing up hundreds or even thousands of miles from Milan or Paris might never visit a flagship boutique, but he could open a magazine and immediately step into another world. A tailored suit, a crisp white shirt, an elegant watch or a bottle of fragrance became symbols of aspiration long before they became purchases. Advertising was not simply describing luxury. It was teaching people how to imagine it.

"Luxury was printed long before it was posted."

Because campaigns remained in circulation for months and sometimes years, every casting decision carried enormous weight. A successful image might appear simultaneously in dozens of countries, translated into multiple languages while keeping the same photograph at its center. The model became the one constant in an international campaign that crossed borders with remarkable efficiency.

Unlike today's digital advertisements, which may disappear within days, print demanded permanence. Magazine deadlines were measured months in advance. Catalogs required long production schedules. Department stores invested heavily in oversized displays designed to withstand an entire retail season. Once released into the marketplace, a campaign developed a life of its own.

That permanence changed how agencies and luxury houses approached casting. Reliability mattered every bit as much as appearance. Creative directors wanted professionals who understood the discipline of commercial work, who could return to another campaign months later and deliver the same confidence that had made the first assignment successful.

It was during this era that commercial modeling matured into a remarkably organized profession. New York, Paris, Milan and London became connected through agencies that understood the growing international demand for dependable talent. A successful campaign in one market often led to another assignment somewhere else. Careers expanded not through celebrity, but through consistency.

As the industry grew, another pattern quietly emerged. Certain faces appeared repeatedly—not because there was a shortage of talent, but because institutional trust had already been established. Men such as John Pearson, Greg Hanson, Jeff Aquilon and Alex Lundqvist demonstrated how professional longevity was built. Their careers reflected a simple reality of luxury advertising: once confidence had been earned, brands were often reluctant to begin again with someone entirely unknown.

The same principle extended beyond individual models. Photographers developed lasting relationships with art directors. Agencies cultivated clients over decades. Fashion houses refined visual identities rather than reinventing them each season. The commercial world valued continuity because continuity created recognition, and recognition built trust.

Department stores understood this instinctively. A customer walking past a display did not have the luxury of studying an advertisement for several minutes. The image had only seconds to communicate confidence, refinement and desire. Every detail mattered—the tailoring, the lighting, the posture, the expression and, above all, the person standing at the center of the frame.

The photograph became more than an advertisement.

It became architecture.

Magazine pages, airport corridors, storefront windows and city billboards formed a continuous visual landscape through which millions of people moved every day. Long before digital algorithms learned how to target individual consumers, print campaigns quietly surrounded them, creating one of the most successful branding systems ever devised.

This was the environment that produced the modern commercial male model. It rewarded professionalism over publicity, discipline over spectacle, and consistency over novelty. Many of the men who defined the decades that followed would emerge from this world, carrying its standards with them even as advertising itself entered a new era.
