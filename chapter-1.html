Chapter One

Before the Supermodel

Every profession begins before anyone notices it exists.

Commercial male modeling was no different.

Long before the term male supermodel entered popular culture, long before social media transformed faces into personal brands, and long before fashion campaigns became headline news, there were men whose careers were built almost entirely out of photographs. They worked without celebrity. They traveled constantly. They represented companies far larger than themselves. Their success was measured not by public recognition, but by the confidence that photographers, agencies, creative directors, and clients placed in them.

Most people never knew their names.

The industry did.

Commercial male modeling emerged from a world that no longer exists. During the decades following the Second World War, international commerce expanded at an extraordinary pace. Department stores grew into retail empires. Fashion magazines reached readers across continents. Air travel connected Europe, North America, and Asia in ways that had never before been possible. Luxury brands suddenly found themselves speaking to audiences far beyond their own countries, and those audiences needed more than products.

They needed images.

Photography became the universal language of aspiration.

A photograph could cross borders more easily than words. A tailored suit needed no translation. Neither did confidence, elegance, or quiet authority. Long before consumers understood the craftsmanship inside a garment or the composition of a fragrance, they responded to the image placed before them.

That image was almost always a person.

Not an actor.

Not an athlete.

Not a celebrity.

A professional model.

Today's audiences often assume modeling has always been tied to fame. In reality, the commercial profession developed in almost the opposite direction. The most successful models were frequently anonymous outside the industry. Their purpose was never to overshadow the brand. They were chosen because they strengthened it. Their faces became familiar while their names remained largely unknown.

That anonymity was not a weakness.

It was an advantage.

Luxury depends on consistency. A campaign succeeds when the public remembers the brand long after the advertisement has disappeared. The model's responsibility was to communicate the character of the house—its confidence, restraint, sophistication, or ambition—without distracting from it. The greatest commercial models understood that distinction instinctively. They were not performing a role. They were giving visual form to an identity that millions of consumers would eventually associate with a fashion house, a fragrance, or an idea of modern masculinity.

The profession matured quietly.

There were no award ceremonies recognizing the year's finest commercial campaign. There were no televised rankings. Success came in different ways. A familiar photographer requested the same model for another assignment. A creative director remembered a face from a campaign completed years earlier. A client renewed a contract instead of searching for someone new. Trust accumulated slowly, assignment by assignment, photograph by photograph.

It was an industry built on reputation long before reputation became something measured by followers or algorithms.

Among the men helping establish that reputation were pioneers whose influence deserves greater recognition today. Sterling St. Jacques brought an unmistakable confidence to fashion photography during the 1970s, becoming one of the era's most important commercial figures. Renauld White demonstrated that elegance and authority could redefine long-held assumptions within luxury advertising, opening doors that had too often remained closed. Bruce Hulse emerged as one of the profession's most dependable presences, while Bob Menna, Nick Constantino, Mike Campbell, John Pearson, and Greg Hanson helped shape an expanding commercial landscape that stretched from magazines and catalogs to department stores and international advertising campaigns.

They represented more than themselves.

They represented a profession still discovering its own identity.

By the late 1970s, agencies had begun to understand that commercial modeling required more than physical appearance. Reliability mattered. Professionalism mattered. The ability to step in front of a camera, understand the purpose of a campaign, and deliver precisely what a client expected often mattered more than conventional ideas of beauty. A successful commercial model might work repeatedly for years, not because he looked like everyone else, but because he could consistently communicate exactly what a brand needed.

This distinction would shape every generation that followed.

As luxury advertising expanded through the 1980s and into the 1990s, the profession itself became more sophisticated. Photographers developed enduring creative partnerships. Agencies grew into international businesses. Fashion houses invested unprecedented resources into building recognizable identities. The commercial male model moved from being a participant in that process to becoming one of its essential foundations.

The public rarely noticed.

History should.
