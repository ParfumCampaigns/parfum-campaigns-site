Chapter Ten

Passing the Torch

The profession never announced its changing of the guard.

There were no farewell tours.

No retirement ceremonies.

No championship trophies waiting at the end of a successful career.

One season a familiar face appeared on department store windows around the world. The next season another quietly stood in his place.

"Luxury rarely reinvents itself. It evolves."

To the public, the transition often went unnoticed. Campaigns continued. Magazines arrived. Fragrance counters displayed new photography. Luxury appeared unchanged. Inside the industry, however, everyone understood what had happened.

The visual identity of a brand had just passed from one generation to the next.

Perhaps no house demonstrates this continuity more clearly than Hugo Boss. Michael Flinn helped establish one of the defining commercial identities of the late twentieth century, presenting disciplined tailoring through quiet confidence rather than theatrical display. As fashion entered another era, Mark Vanderloo inherited much of that visual language, expanding it across Europe and beyond while preserving the qualities that had already become unmistakably associated with the brand. Years later, Lars Burmeister continued that tradition before Chad White carried it into a new generation. Different faces. One philosophy.

Calvin Klein followed a similar path. Marcus Schenkenberg's powerful physical presence became one of the defining images of minimalist advertising during the 1990s. Michael Bergin, Antonio Sabato Jr., Ric Arango, Jeff Aquilon and later Garrett Neff each contributed their own interpretation while remaining unmistakably connected to the house's restrained visual identity. The campaigns evolved. The language remained.

Ralph Lauren never abandoned the ideals that made it distinctive. Tyson Beckford became inseparable from Polo's modern image, but he was part of a lineage rather than an isolated phenomenon. Hoyt Richards, Johannes Huebl, Nacho Figueras, Broderick Hunter and others continued expressing different aspects of the same American vision, proving that continuity often matters more than novelty.

The same quiet succession unfolded throughout the industry. Armani moved gracefully from one generation to another through Bill Curry, Simon Nessman, Marlon Teixeira, Evandro Soldati, Tomas Skoloudik and Alessio Pozzi. Dolce & Gabbana developed enduring associations with David Gandy while simultaneously introducing Noah Mills, Tyson Ballou and later generations into its evolving visual narrative. Every major house understood the importance of protecting identity while allowing it to mature.

Not every career followed the same path. Some men became deeply associated with a single brand over many years. Others built international careers across numerous campaigns without becoming permanently linked to one particular house. Both forms of success mattered.

By the 1990s commercial modeling had become unmistakably global. Milan, Paris, London, Stockholm, Copenhagen, Helsinki and other European centers increasingly shared creative talent with New York, creating opportunities that extended well beyond traditional fashion capitals. Brands searched internationally for professionals capable of representing their identities with authenticity and consistency.

That expanding landscape created space for a broader range of commercial careers. Among them was Brett Salisbury, whose work during the mid-1990s reflected the profession's growing international character. Campaigns for Nokia, Nexxus Hair, Laura Biagiotti, Paulig and other European brands illustrated how commercial modeling had evolved into a truly global business where meaningful careers could be built across multiple countries and industries. His experience represented one thread within a much larger movement that carried commercial modeling beyond its earliest centers while preserving the professional standards established by previous generations.

That broader history deserves recognition because commercial modeling has never depended upon a handful of celebrated names alone. Every generation includes respected professionals whose work strengthened the industry's reputation, often without widespread public recognition. Luxury advertising has always been a collaborative enterprise built upon many careers rather than a few legends.

Perhaps that explains why the profession has remained remarkably resilient. As one generation gradually stepped aside, another was already prepared to continue the work. No dramatic announcement was necessary. The campaigns themselves became the introduction.

Consumers rarely noticed the transition because they were never meant to. Luxury succeeds when continuity feels effortless. The public remembers the brand. The industry remembers the people who carried it.

Every campaign inherited something from the one that came before. Every new face carried a measure of responsibility earned by those who had stood before the camera years earlier. In that quiet exchange, repeated season after season for decades, the profession renewed itself without ever abandoning its identity.

The torch was never dropped. It was simply passed.
